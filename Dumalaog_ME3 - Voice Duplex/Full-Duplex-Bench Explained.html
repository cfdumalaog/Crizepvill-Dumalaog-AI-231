Full-Duplex-Bench, explained

The paper asks: does a voice AI know when to talk and when to stay quiet? It answers with 10 numbers per model. Part 1 explains those 10 measures with pictures. Part 2 shows the test data and how four systems scored on each.

Lin, Lian, Li, Wang, Anumanchipalli, Liu, Lee. All numbers are copied from the paper's Tables II and III and checked against the paper text. Notes marked "inferred" or "ours" are not the paper's.

Before the measures · how any score is made

Play a recording in, record the reply, read the word times

A test clip of a person speaking is streamed into the model in real time, as if the person were on the line. The model's reply is recorded on its own, time-synced track. A speech-to-text model (ASR; here NVIDIA Parakeet) writes down every word the model said, with the time each word started and ended. Every one of the 10 measures is computed from those word times.

Candor
850 hours of spontaneous, open-ended two-person conversations, each person on their own channel. Pause set: turns longer than 5 s with a 0.4–1.0 s pause inside, no "mm-hm" next to the pause. Turn-taking set: clean handoffs (gap under 0.4 s, no overlap, turns over 4 s), with 5 s of silence added at the end so the model has room to reply. All hand-checked.
ICC
28.33 minutes of informal American English dialogue. Backchannel responses collected from 118 native speakers, about 59 per clip. That crowd pattern is the answer key for Freq and JSD.
Synthetic
GPT-4o writes the dialogue; a text-to-speech system (ChatTTS, 10 voices) speaks it. Pauses are inserted on purpose with a break tag. For interruptions, the interrupting sentence starts about 7 s in, then 15 s of silence follow.
Honest limits. English only. 55 backchannel clips is small, so one clip moves TOR by about 0.018. Synthetic voices are cleaner than a real mic. The paper does not test whether its scores match what people actually prefer.

How the four models scored

dGSLM & Moshi

Turn audio straight into audio with no text step in between (end-to-end). No explicit "is it my turn?" logic.

Freeze-Omni & Gemini Live

Explicit turn logic. Freeze-Omni: a voice-activity detector (VAD, which decides when someone is speaking) plus a classifier that predicts the dialogue state. Gemini Live: VAD on Google's server, via the API.

A trade-off between speed and manners

Read it
Rows are models, columns are the 10 measures. Arrows show which direction is good. Within each column, green marks the best of the four and red the worst. Colour compares models within a column; it doesn't say how good a score is on its own.
Result
Eager talkers (dGSLM, Moshi): fast, but they talk over pauses and give weak answers. Careful thinkers (Freeze-Omni, Gemini Live): wait more and answer better, but take about 1 s or more to reply.
Precision notes. All values are averages; the paper gives no median, spread or confidence interval, so small gaps (0.934 vs 0.935) mean nothing. The paper doesn't specify the time window in which a takeover is checked for each test, nor the GPT-4o judge prompt; those live in the released code. Scores also depend on the ASR: a misheard "mm-hm" can turn a 0 into a 1.
Table I · why only four models

17 full-duplex models, few you can actually test

Full-duplex means the model can listen and talk at the same moment, like a phone call, unlike a walkie-talkie. Of the 17 listed, only dGSLM, Moshi and Freeze-Omni release a working speech-in, speech-out system; with Gemini's API, those are the four tested. E2E = end-to-end (no text step); #ch = audio input channels; BC = produces backchannels.

For our Pi 4 assistant

What this means for our project

Our pipeline (Silero VAD, cloud ASR, LLM, cloud TTS, via MCP) is the same kind of design as Freeze-Omni and Gemini Live: separate steps with explicit turn logic. Expect their profile: decent manners and answers, with latency as the main fight.

Direct lessons

  • Our 1 s target is ambitious but fair. Gemini's turn-taking latency is 1.30 s and Freeze-Omni's 0.95 s, on their own servers.
  • The VAD silence setting is the main dial. Candor's test pauses are 0.4–1.0 s. Pipecat's default VAD stop time is 0.8 s, so longer pauses will trigger a takeover. Raising it helps pause handling but adds directly to latency.
  • Fast barge-in isn't enough. Moshi shows a quick reaction is worthless if the reply ignores what the user said.

How our latency differs

Their latency uses ASR word times on a clean digital stream. Ours is measured on the sound itself in a stereo recording (end of prompt audio to first reply audio), through a real mic, network and speaker. Ours includes the whole device chain, so the two aren't directly comparable. We also report median and p90; they report means only.

Proposed use of their dataset

keepOur 20 prompts for voice-to-voice latency (median, p90). addPause handling, synthetic (137 clips): play into the Pi, report column 1. Shows whether the bot cuts users off mid-thought, and helps tune VAD stop time. addUser interruption (200 clips, or a 20–30 clip sample): report columns 8 and 10, plus our barge-in stop time from the same recording. addSmooth turn-taking, Candor (119 clips): report columns 6 and 7, so our dot sits on the same chart as Gemini and Freeze-Omni. skipBackchannel (columns 3–5): our bot doesn't say "mm-hm" by design, so Freq and JSD would be trivially bad. Optionally report column 3 only, as a "doesn't talk over you" check.

Practical notes: run the scoring (Parakeet ASR, metric scripts) on a laptop or cloud machine, not the Pi. Check the licence terms for the Candor and ICC clips before putting them in the repo or demo video.
